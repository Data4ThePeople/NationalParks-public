# Datasets

One section per dataset, written before analysis and kept current. This file
is generated by `scripts/build_datasets_md.py` from `research/datasets/*.md`;
edit those files, not this one.

## Method: what the page computes

These are the rules the page follows. Every number is computed in the browser
from the processed files, and `scripts/tieout.py` recomputes each one in Python.

**Parks.** The 63 units with "National Park" in their name, from the NPS Land
Resources Division boundary layer (62 typed "National Park," plus New River
Gorge National Park and Preserve). States come from that layer's STATE field.

**Visits.** Recreation visits from IRMA, summed by calendar year. Only years
with all 12 months reported count. The last full year is 2025. 2026 months are
preliminary and are not shown. The visit chart opens at 2008, the first budget year, like every other
series; a switch shows the full record back to 1979 (or the park's first year).

**Budget.** Park base budget, as published in thousands of dollars, from the
"actual" (or "final") column of each budget book. The book for FY N gives FY N-2.
Shared budget lines (`config/budget_units.json`) show the line's figure on each
park it covers. Kobuk Valley has no budget figures shown, because its shared line
changed membership in FY2020.

**Inflation.** Dollars are converted to FY2025 dollars with CPI-U (BLS series
CUUR0000SA0), averaged over each federal fiscal year (October to September):
`real = nominal × CPI(FY2025) / CPI(FY)`.

**Staff.** Total FTE as published: every position at the park, whatever money
pays for it. An FTE is regular hours worked (with paid leave counted as hours
worked) divided by the paid hours in a fiscal year (OMB Circular A-11, 1999
edition, section 32.1,
https://clintonwhitehouse4.archives.gov/media/pdf/s32_1.pdf). The budget books
do not define it further.

**Visit counting changes.** The panel counts the rows in IRMA's "Park Visitor
Use and Count Procedure Changes" report from 1979 through the last full year that NPS rates Overhaul,
Major or Minor. Rows rated Non-material (label and wording updates) are not
counted. Overhaul and Major changes are drawn as dashed lines.

**Budget per visit.** Real base budget for fiscal year N divided by recreation
visits in calendar year N, summed over every park on the budget line. Shown only
where every unit on the line is one of the 63 parks (each park's own line, plus
Sequoia and Kings Canyon together). Katmai's and North Cascades' lines also fund
units outside the 63, so they have no per-visit figure.

**Changes on the map, in the table and in the panel sentences.** Percent change
`(later / earlier − 1) × 100`:
- visits, 2012 to 2025
- real base budget, FY2012 to FY2023
- total FTE, FY2012 to FY2023
- real budget per visit, FY2012 to FY2023

FY2012 is the comparison year because the FY2011 and FY2012 books moved human
resources and purchasing money from park lines to central lines, and FY2013 was
the sequester year. Bryce Canyon has no visit or per-visit change, and no budget-per-visit chart,
because NPS says its 2000 to 2024 visits were too high by about 18 to 19 percent
and 2025 is not affected.

**Rounding.** Half away from zero, everywhere, including the stored CPI averages. Percent changes are whole numbers. Money of
$1 million or more is shown in millions with one decimal; per-visit money is
shown to the cent. A change that rounds to 0% shows no sign.

**Map bins.** The bins apply to the whole-number change the table shows, so a
park's color always matches its table value. These are percent change bins:
- down 20% or more
- more than 5% down, up to 20% down
- within 5% either way
- 5% up to 20% up
- 20% up or more

The exact edges are: v ≤ −20; −20 < v ≤ −5; −5 < v < 5; 5 ≤ v < 20; v ≥ 20. Dot area grows with visits in 2025.

**Deferred maintenance.** Not shown on the page (taken off on 2026-10-22). The
dataset is documented below for the post, and what is missing from it is in
the page's "What is missing" box.

**Park summaries.** Built by `scripts/build_viz.py` from `config/park_history.json`,
one sentence per dated event, then the size from the NPS acreage report (three
significant figures). See the rules in `research/tieout_words.md`, item 26.

## Rules that apply to every dataset

- No gap filling, no smoothing, no hand-typed values. Gaps stay gaps.
- A duplicate key, an unparseable number or an unknown park stops the build.
- Raw downloads are cached in `data/raw/` and treated as untrusted data.
- "No longer published" means we can show the data was published before and
  is absent now. "Not available" means we found no evidence it was ever
  published. We state facts only and do not guess at reasons. The evidence is
  in `research/availability/`.

## Visitation

### NPS Visitor Use Statistics (National Park Service, IRMA)

**What it is.** The National Park Service (NPS) count of visits to each park,
reported every month by the park and compiled by the NPS Social Science
Program in its IRMA system. Each row in `data/processed/visits_monthly.csv` is
one park-month: recreation visits and non-recreation visits as IRMA publishes
them. `visits_annual.csv` sums the monthly recreation visits by calendar year.

- A **recreation visit** is "The entry of a person onto lands or waters
  administered by the NPS except for non-reportable and non-recreation
  visits." The rule is one entrance per person per day, so one person who
  enters on three days counts as three visits. A visit is not the same as a
  visitor.
  ([definitions](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/nps-visitor-use-statistics-definitions.htm),
  [FAQ](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/statistics-faq.htm))
- A **non-recreation visit** is a visit "for their own convenience or as a part
  of their occupation": commuters and through traffic on park roads, people
  going to and from private land inside the park, tradespeople, guides,
  government workers other than NPS staff, and outside researchers.
- **Non-reportable** entries are left out of both: NPS staff and their
  families, concession and contractor staff, volunteers, and brief passing
  traffic. ([Statistical Abstract 2025, Definitions, pp. ix to xi](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/DownloadFile/759491))

**Where it comes from.** Three IRMA outlets, no login needed.

1. REST service, XML or JSON:
   `https://irmaservices.nps.gov/v3/rest/stats/visitation?unitCodes=YELL&startMonth=1&startYear=1979&endMonth=12&endYear=2026`.
   It accepts comma-separated codes (`unitCodes=YELL,ZION`) and a full
   1979 to 2026 range in one call. It returns XML by default and JSON with
   `format=json` or an `Accept: application/json` header. Fields: Month,
   NonRecreationVisitors, RecreationVisitors, UnitCode, UnitName, Year. A
   second operation, `total/{year}`, returns system-wide monthly totals. An
   unknown code returns an empty array with HTTP 200, not an error. We fetch
   one park per call and cache it in `data/raw/irma/<CODE>.xml` (63 files,
   about 120 KB each).
2. The IRMA Stats portal, https://irma.nps.gov/Stats/, which serves SQL Server
   Reporting Services reports. Each report has a CSV export. We use
   "Monthly Public Use" (one park and one month per export, parameters
   `Park=<CODE>&RptDate=<M>/1/<YYYY>`) for current-year months, cached in
   `data/raw/irma/prelim/`. We use "Annual Park Recreation Visitation (1904 -
   Last Calendar Year)" only to check our sums (`scripts/check_irma_annual.py`,
   cache in `data/raw/irma_annual_report/`).
3. The "NPS Visitor Use Statistics Data Package, 2025" on data.gov
   (https://catalog.data.gov/dataset/nps-visitor-use-statistics-data-package-2025),
   issued 2026-03-26, covering 1979-01-01 to 2025-12-31, as two CSV files and
   an XML metadata file on the NPS DataStore (reference 2317666). We did not
   use it; it ends at 2025.

Scripts: `scripts/fetch/irma_visits.py` (visits) and
`scripts/fetch/irma_method_changes.py` (count procedure changes). Both take
`--refresh`; `irma_visits.py` also takes `--final-only` to skip the
current-year months.

**Version and vintage.** Pulled 2026-10-22. Parks enter preliminary data every
month, and "most data are available by the 15th of the following month." The
preliminary data "may be edited by the park until the entire calendar year is
finalized and moved to permanent tables in the first quarter of the following
year" ([FAQ](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/statistics-faq.htm)).
The IRMA Stats home page on 2026-10-22 said: "Current calendar year (2026) data
are preliminary and subject to change. Data will be finalized by the end of the
first quarter of next calendar year (2027)." The 2025 totals were announced on
2026-03-13 ([NPS news release](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/1207/03-13-26-2025-visitation-statsitics.htm)),
and the 2025 Statistical Abstract has an issue date of June 2026 in the
[IRMA abstracts list](https://irma.nps.gov/Stats/Reports/AbstractsAndForecasts).

As of 2026-10-22 the REST service returns nothing for 2026 (empty array for
every park we tried, and for `total/2026`). Its last month is December 2025
for all 63 parks. The "Monthly Public Use" report does return January through
September 2026 for each park. Our monthly file therefore holds:

- 1979 through 2025: final values from the REST service.
- January through September 2026: preliminary values from "Monthly Public
  Use" (518 park-months). These will change. Parks post on their own
  schedule, so the count differs by park: 46 parks have January through
  September, and the rest stop earlier (Denali through March; Crater Lake,
  American Samoa and Olympic through July; 10 parks through August).
  Channel Islands, Katmai and Lake Clark have no 2026 month posted as of
  2026-10-22. The report's own date list for each park shows which months are
  posted, and the script reads only those. `visits_annual.csv` gives each
  park's 2026 row with its `months_reported`. Run with `--final-only` to leave
  2026 out.

**Coverage.** 63 national parks, January 1979 through September 2026 (2026 is
preliminary). Monthly data begin in 1979 for every park whose history in IRMA
reaches back that far. Eight parks start later in IRMA:

| Park | First month in IRMA |
|---|---|
| GAAR, KEFJ, KOVA, LACL, WRST | January 1982 |
| NERI | January 1984 |
| CONG | January 1985 |
| NPSA | January 2002 |

Many of these units existed under other names or types before they became
national parks (see `config/designations.json`). IRMA files the history under
the current code, so a series can include years before the unit was a
national park. For example, Gateway Arch (JEFF) data from 1979 are for the
Jefferson National Expansion Memorial.

All values are estimates built from counts. The FAQ says "Counts can be
automated, such as with traffic counters, door counters, or trail counters,
or they can be done manually by hand counting." In parks with roads, "often
just the number of vehicles is counted. Vehicle counts are combined with
person-per-vehicle multipliers to estimate the number of visits." The
multipliers "can vary by park, by location within a park, by time of year,
and by type of vehicle." Counts from one area can be used to estimate visits
to remote areas "where needed and appropriate." The per-park "Visitor Use
Counting and Reporting Instructions" spell this out: which counter, how the
count is divided (often by 2 for entering and leaving), how many staff
vehicles are taken off each month, and the persons-per-vehicle multiplier.
For example, Arches multiplies its adjusted traffic count by "2.4 persons per
vehicle September through January or 2.9 persons per vehicle February through
August" (instructions dated January 2024). Many instructions also say how to
fill days when a counter is broken: under 14 days out, use the month's own
average daily traffic; 14 or more days out, use a historic monthly value from
a table in the instructions (Arches, Great Smoky Mountains, Hawaiʻi
Volcanoes).

The publisher does not report what share of each value comes from a direct
count, a multiplier, or a fill-in. Park comments (see Missing data) show that
fill-ins are common: 5-year averages, last year's numbers, or a share of
another counter.

**Changes over time.** Each park works under its own instructions. The NPS
says they "are applicable beginning January 1 of the year of issue (except for
newly added parks) and remain in effect until and unless they are changed by
agreement between the park's superintendent and the Social Science Program"
([program page](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics.htm)).
The NPS warns that a change "can result in trend shift in the years following
a change" ([data page](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics-dashboard.htm)).

Changes are flagged per park and per year in two places:

- The IRMA report "Park Visitor Use and Count Procedure Changes" lists, for
  each set of instructions, a level (Overhaul, Major, Minor, Non-material) and
  one line per change. `config/visit_method_changes.json` is built from it by
  script: 301 park-years with at least one listed change, across 61 parks.
  Big Bend and American Samoa list no described change. The PDF of every set
  of instructions is also on the park's IRMA page ("Visitor Use Counting
  Instructions"); we downloaded all 362 PDFs for the 63 parks. Each is dated
  on its first page, mostly "January <year>".
- The Statistical Abstract endnotes list each year's count procedure changes
  and closures by park, and an "Errors in previous years" list.

The change years and the document periods mostly agree. Exceptions: the
change report lists Rocky Mountain 2021 and Mount Rainier 1995 and 1996, which
are not start years in the document list; Yellowstone has a "2022 - Present"
row with no details; and the Katmai "2024 - Present" PDF is dated January 2021
on its first page. 32 parks have early document periods (mostly before 2000)
with no described change.

Six parks have new instructions dated 2026 (ARCH, CANY, GAAR, HAVO, VIIS,
YOSE), so their preliminary 2026 months use a different method from 2025.

Unit names and types also change: 26 of the 63 units became national parks in
1978 or later, and some were renamed (Lafayette to Acadia, Mt. McKinley to
Denali, General Grant to Kings Canyon). The REST service returns only the
current name for every year. `config/designations.json` has the year each
unit became a national park, with sources.

**Suppressed, censored or masked values.** None documented. Values are whole
numbers with no suppression codes.

**Missing data.** The REST service has no missing-value code. A month with no
report is simply absent. Inside each park's range we found:

- Katmai (KATM): all of 1996 absent.
- American Samoa (NPSA): all of 2004 and 2005 absent.
- Channel Islands (CHIS): December 2005 absent, so 2005 has 11 months.

The published annual report shows the same gaps (no KATM 1996 row, no NPSA
2004 or 2005 row). We leave these gaps as gaps.

A zero is a published value, not a missing one. 409 park-months from 1979 to
2025 have zero recreation visits, mostly in Alaska parks in winter (Katmai 97,
Gates of the Arctic 83) and Isle Royale (68). In 2020, 46
park-months are zero, mostly April and May.

Closures and shutdowns are reported by each park in its own way. The
publisher does not apply one rule. The optional "Monthly Visitation Comments
By Park" report (comments start in 2003 or 2004 for most parks) shows what
parks did:

- October 1 to 16, 2013: many parks closed. Comments say visits were low or
  zero during the closure (Yellowstone: "between Oct 1-16 there were no
  recreational visitor entries"). Sequoia used 3-year averages "multiplied by
  .50 to represent shutdown."
- December 22, 2018 to January 25, 2019: many parks stayed partly open with no
  staff at entrances. Comments describe fill-ins: 5-year averages (Everglades,
  Theodore Roosevelt, Voyageurs), splitting a two-month count in half (Great
  Sand Dunes), and estimates from last year (Bryce Canyon, Wind Cave). Zion:
  "ALL THESE NUMBERS ARE NOT ACCURATE DUE TO GOVERNMENT SHUTDOWN FROM JANUARY
  1st - 27th." Joshua Tree: "Lapse in appropriation prevented the collection
  of accurate data."
- October 1 to November 12, 2025 (a 43-day lapse in appropriations, per the
  NPS release): the 2025 Abstract endnotes list parks "Closed" or "Partial
  closure October 1 through November 12 due to lapse in appropriations,"
  including Denali, Isle Royale, Katmai and Petrified Forest (partial or full)
  and American Samoa (closed). Park comments say bus, non-recreation and
  non-reportable inputs were estimated from 2024 data at Yellowstone and
  Arches, and Grand Canyon wrote: "Estimates made for first two weeks and
  added to official numbers."
- 2020: the 2020 Abstract says "methods for collecting and estimating visitor
  use statistics at many parks had to be altered for 2020, and that official
  statistics reported here may be more approximate than usual," and that "66
  parks in the National Park System were fully closed for two months or more."
  Its Appendix A lists pandemic closures by park.

A late value does fill in within the current year, since parks can edit
preliminary months until the year is finalized.

**Revisions.** The NPS says: "Once the calendar year is over and data for a
given year is finalized, no changes to the underlying values can be made.
Some parks' data may contain permanent errors. Errors are noted in the
Endnotes of the Annual Statistical Abstracts"
([data page](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics-dashboard.htm)).
So final years are not revised, even when an error is found later, and a
method change is not applied back to earlier years. Current-year months do
change until the year is final. We refresh the whole series on each run and
treat 2026 as preliminary.

Errors the Abstracts list that touch recreation visits at our 63 parks (from
the 2019 to 2025 Abstracts; hours-only and overnight-only errors left out):

- Bryce Canyon: "From 2000 through 2024, there was an error in the number of
  shuttle bus passengers entering the park from Bryce Canyon City—this caused
  reported recreation visits to be too high by approximately 18–19 percent
  each year" (2025 Abstract). IRMA still shows the uncorrected values: BRCA
  was 2,498,075 in 2024 and 1,967,367 in 2025.
- Haleakalā: "From 2007 through 2018, miscellaneous fee visits were
  incorrectly added to recreation visits" (2019 Abstract).
- Guadalupe Mountains: McKittrick Canyon traffic "should have been divided by
  two for entry and exit; unclear how long this error has existed, perhaps
  since 1992" (2019).
- Denali: Talkeetna counter "not operating correctly in summer 2017 resulting
  in a doubling of summer 2017 data at that location" (2019).
- Zion: bus passenger multipliers "switched between south entrance and east
  entrance / Kolob Canyons from 2013 through 2018" (2019).
- Lassen Volcanic: "From 2008 through 2018, non-reportable vehicles at
  southwest entrance were inadvertently omitted from calculations" (2019).
- Canyonlands: "Some bus passenger data in the past has been skewed down"
  (2019). Everglades: "Some data were missing or delayed in 2018 which
  resulted in under-reporting for the year" (2019).
- Congaree: Bannister Bridge count "should have been divided by 2 from 2012
  through 2021" (2022). New River Gorge: Sandstone Falls and Glade Creek
  "should have been divided by 2 for entry and exit, unclear how long this
  error has been occurring" (2021).
- Mount Rainier: September estimates at Mather Wye and Cayuse Pass were 100
  percent of the traffic count "from 1995 through 2022 – it should have been
  30%" (2023). Rocky Mountain: Beaver Meadows "reported too high by 12,982
  vehicles in July 2022" (2023).

Abstract PDFs: [2019](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/DownloadFile/637876),
[2021](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/DownloadFile/672277),
[2022](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/DownloadFile/689245),
[2023](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/DownloadFile/704975),
[2025](https://irma.nps.gov/DataStore/DownloadFile/759491).

**Units and rounding.** Visits (count of entries), whole numbers. The data
page notes that "data users may notice slight rounding discrepancies across
different reports." We did not find one in the final years: all 2,909
park-year sums match the annual report exactly. Preliminary months do move:
Yellowstone's January to September 2026 total was 4,360,296 in a "Monthly
Public Use" export at about 10:15 on 2026-10-22 and 4,360,300 in our run a few
hours later. Values are published to the single visit,
but they come from vehicle counts times multipliers such as 2.4, so the real
precision is much coarser than one visit.

**Known quirks.**

- Negative non-recreation values are published and kept: Capitol Reef July
  2011 (-788), Hawaiʻi Volcanoes October 2005 (-32,964), and six Mount Rainier
  months in 2002 and 2003 (-16 to -112). Recreation visits have no negatives.
- IRMA UnitName uses short forms ("Yellowstone NP") and writes "Hawaii
  Volcanoes NP" without the ʻokina. The script checks names after expanding
  the short forms and dropping apostrophes. All 63 match, and each code has
  one name across all years.
- The REST service gives HTTP 200 and an empty array for an unknown code or
  for a period with no final data, so an empty answer is not an error message.
- The current-year months come from a different report than the final years.
  The script checks that each report's "same month last year" value equals the
  REST value for that month, so the two outlets agree on 2025.
- Bryce Canyon 2000 to 2024 is too high by the publisher's own statement (see
  Revisions).
- Some monthly values are fill-ins after counter failures or shutdowns (see
  Missing data). There is no flag in the data for these months.

**Uncertainty.** The NPS gives no error bars or confidence ranges for any
park or year. Its statements about accuracy are qualitative:

- Parks "must sometimes rely on estimated data due to equipment failures,
  staffing limitations, and unforeseeable circumstances such as the COVID-19
  pandemic and natural disasters," and updating counting methods "can take a
  great deal of time and financial resources," which makes it "challenging to
  keep measurement systems completely current and accurate"
  ([data page](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/socialscience/visitor-use-statistics-dashboard.htm)).
- 2020 statistics "may be more approximate than usual" (2020 Abstract).
- Current-year data "may not be complete or controlled for quality."
- Abstracts from 2013 to 2020 say "Care has been taken to assure accuracy of
  raw data values, but a thorough analysis and interpretation of the data has
  not been completed."

## Budget

### Park base funding and staff (NPS budget justifications, "Green Books")

**What it is.** Each year the National Park Service (NPS) sends Congress a
budget justification, which NPS calls the Green Book. Each book has a table of
each park and program in the Operation of the National Park System (ONPS)
account. The table is called the "Park and Program Summary" in the FY 2010 to
FY 2021 books and the "Park and Program Table" in the FY 2022 to FY 2025
books. Each row in `data/processed/budget.csv` is one budget line in one year:

- `park_base_k`: the park's base operating funding, in thousands of dollars,
  from the book's final or actual column for that year.
- `park_base_fte`: staff paid from park base funding, counted in full-time
  equivalents (FTE).
- `total_fte`: all staff at the park, from any fund source, counted in FTE.

The book for fiscal year N reports the final or actual amount for FY N-2, so
the FY 2025 book gives FY 2023 amounts. We keep only that column. The other
columns (enacted, continuing resolution, request) are plans for later years.

Plain-word definitions, from the table footnotes:

- **Park base.** The footnote on the "Total, Park Base" line says NPS "uses
  these totals when responding to inquiries as to the amount of funding
  directly available for 'park base operations.' Items which follow this total
  also support park operations, but are managed at the Regional or Servicewide
  level" (for example, FY 2014 book, footnote 7, PDF page 199; FY 2025 book,
  footnote 4, PDF page 74). The FY 2010 book says this base funding "is under
  the direct control of the park superintendent" (PDF page 199).
- **What is not in park base.** The table covers only the ONPS account. Other
  money that a park may spend is in other accounts the books list separately,
  such as Construction, Recreation Fee Programs and Donations (FY 2025 book,
  Discretionary and Mandatory Appropriations tables, PDF pages 47 to 51). The
  notes to those tables also list Great American Outdoors Act and Inflation
  Reduction Act amounts outside ONPS (PDF page 52). The
  FY 2011 table shows American Recovery and Reinvestment Act money as one
  separate total line ($146,000K), not in park rows (PDF page 245). Lines after
  "Total, Park Base" in the same table, such as regional offices, servicewide
  programs, cyclic maintenance and repair project funds, the Servicing Human
  Resources Offices, the Major Acquisition Buying Offices, and External
  Administrative Costs (central bills such as GSA rent), are also not in any
  park's base. So a park's base is a part of what is spent at the park, not
  all of it.
- **Park base FTE vs. total FTE.** Footnote 1: park base FTE "Represents
  Full-Time Equivalents (FTE) funded from park base operating dollars."
  Footnote 2: "Total FTE shown for parks are by organization, irrespective of
  funding source. For example, some temporary positions in parks are funded
  from construction, recreation fees, etc." (FY 2025 book, PDF page 74; the
  same wording, with small changes, is in every book from FY 2011 on). An FTE
  is a count of work hours turned into full-time jobs, so two half-time
  workers count as one FTE. The books do not define FTE further.
- **Final, actual, enacted and CR.** Through the FY 2020 book the column is
  headed "Final" (FY 2010 book: "FINAL"). From the FY 2021 book it is headed
  "Actual". The footnotes describe both the same way: "The Actual amounts
  reflect presentation adjustments or shifts of funds within reprogramming
  guidelines. Amounts may vary from previously listed levels" (FY 2025 book,
  footnote 5). The FY 2023 and FY 2024 books head the column "Actual" but the
  footnote calls it "Final." Neither word appears to mean money spent. In the
  one case we checked, the FY 2025 book's FY 2023 "Actual" total for ONPS
  ($2,923,424K, PDF page 74) is the same as the FY 2024 book's FY 2023
  "Enacted" total (PDF page 64). The FY 2012 book's FY 2010 "Final" total and
  its FY 2010 "Adjusted Enacted" total are also the same ($2,261,559K, PDF page
  264). So these figures look like each park's share of the enacted
  appropriation after any approved shifts, not its spending. "Enacted" is the
  amount in the year's appropriations law. "CR" is a continuing resolution,
  a short-term law that keeps funding at about the prior year's level when no
  full-year law has passed.

**Where it comes from.** PDFs linked from
https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/budget.htm (checked 2026-10-22). FY 2010 to FY 2018
are on nps.gov (`/aboutus/upload/FY_<year>_NPS_Greenbook_508.pdf`; FY 2016 is
`FY_2016_NPS_Greenbook_508-2.pdf`). FY 2019 is a zip on nps.gov

## Deferred maintenance

### NPS deferred maintenance and repairs, by park (National Park Service; Department of the Interior)

**What it is.** Deferred maintenance and repairs (DM&R) is the National Park
Service (NPS) estimate of what it would cost to fix the park facilities that
need work that was put off. NPS says DM&R "is an estimate of how much it would
cost to repair and restore deteriorating facilities to an acceptable and safe
condition," and that it "is created when routine maintenance and repairs are
delayed because of limited resources"
([NPS FAQ](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/infrastructure/faqs.htm), last updated
May 9, 2022). The Department of the Interior (DOI) uses the federal accounting
definition: "a maintenance and repair activity that was not performed when it
should have been or was scheduled to be performed but was delayed to a future
period" ([DOI DM&R page](https://www.doi.gov/deferred-maintenance-and-repair)).

DM&R counts only the cost to correct existing problems. The FY2024 and FY2025
park fact sheets say it "does not include, for example, alterations for
improved accessibility, upgrades to meet current building codes, installation
of new fire suppression systems, expansion of capacity, or annual recurring
maintenance needs." It is a snapshot of the backlog at the end of a fiscal year
(September 30), not money spent.

Each row of `data/processed/deferred_maintenance.csv` is one park and one
fiscal year: the DM&R dollars NPS (or DOI, or the Congressional Research
Service quoting NPS) published for that park's reporting unit, with the URL and
capture date of the copy we read and a note on the method in use that year.

**Where it comes from.** No single file covers all years. The script
`scripts/fetch/deferred_maintenance.py` reads five official sources:

| Fiscal years | Source | Format | Precision | Where we read it |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2014-2018 | NPS "Deferred Maintenance by State and Park" reports | PDF, 7 to 12 pages, about 160-300 KB | whole dollars | Wayback Machine copies of files NPS removed from nps.gov (for example [FY2018](https://web.archive.org/web/20190309155240/https://www.nps.gov/subjects/infrastructure/upload/NPS-Deferred-Maintenance-FY18-State_and_Park_2018.pdf)) |
| 2022 | NPS "DM&R Park Fact Sheets" sortable dataset | JSON and CSV behind an nps.gov table | whole dollars | Wayback copies of every version, 2023-2026 |
| 2023 | CRS Report R48136, Table 4, top 20 units "using data from NPS" | HTML | $1 million | [everycrsreport.com copy, July 25, 2024](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2024-07-25_R48136_b8f735eff6a455e0ad5cff787c7348b59f659b10.html) |
| 2024 | NPS park "Infrastructure Fact Sheet" PDFs | PDF, 2 pages | 2 or 3 significant figures ("$161 M") | Wayback copies; NPS has since replaced each file with the FY2025 version at the same URL |
| 2025 | DOI unit-level DM&R workbook; NPS park fact sheets | XLSX (about 1,700 rows); PDF | cents (DOI); 2 or 3 significant figures (NPS) | [DOI workbook](https://www.doi.gov/sites/default/files/documents/2026-07/gaoa-pmofy25-categorized-unit-level-dm-r.xlsx), live NPS fact sheets |

**Version and vintage.** NPS reports DM&R once a year, as of September 30.
NPS wrote in 2019-2022 that "only final, year-end figures are reported to
ensure a consistent reference point" and that it "does not report 'live' data
or update reports on request"
([2019 capture](https://web.archive.org/web/20190309155227/https://www.nps.gov/subjects/infrastructure/identifying-reporting-deferred-maintenance.htm)).
Two exceptions matter here:

- In 2022 NPS posted a park dataset labeled Fiscal Year 2022 Quarter 1 (total
  $21.8 billion, used in a [May 9, 2022 news release](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/1207/05-09-2022-dm-r.htm)).
  It is a mid-year snapshot. The build caches it but does not write it, because
  it is not a year-end figure. The values for our parks are listed in
  `research/dm_overlap.md`.
- The FY2023 NPS-wide total was first posted as $23,263 million (captured
  2024-05-01) and now reads $23,258 million on the FY2023-FY2025 history table.
  The FY2023 location count went from 71,808 to 71,805, and the Intermountain
  Region from $5,087 million to $5,082 million.

Park fact sheets are replaced in place: the URL that held the FY2024 sheet now
holds the FY2025 sheet. Earlier years survive only in archived copies.

**Coverage.** FY2014-FY2018, FY2022 and FY2025 for all 63 parks. FY2024 for
25 parks: the Wayback Machine holds an FY2024 copy of the fact sheet for only
those parks: Acadia through Dry Tortugas in alphabetical order, plus Glacier,
Grand Teton, Great Smoky Mountains, Haleakala, Rocky Mountain, Sequoia and
Kings Canyon (two rows), and Yellowstone. FY2023 values for nine of our parks appear only in CRS's top-20 table (rounded to $1 million); the build leaves them out, so no FY2023 value is shown. We found no park-level figures for FY2019, FY2020 or FY2021 from
NPS, DOI, CRS or GAO. CRS lists NPS-wide totals for those years, from "CRS
communication with NPS Office of Legislative and Congressional Affairs, July
27, 2023" ([R48136, Oct. 15, 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-10-15_R48136_27b01d0b7d1a8481d148a6971daf90361ed6820d.html)).

Every value is an estimate built from asset records, not a measurement. Park
staff record asset condition in NPS software, and the software turns condition
and deficiency records into dollar estimates. The share that comes from each
method changed over time (see the next section). The DOI Office of Inspector
General found in 2023 that the data in the NPS system were "inaccurate and
unreliable," which "cast[s] doubt on the NPS' deferred maintenance
estimates," possibly "underestimating the figures in some cases and
overestimating in others" (as quoted in CRS R48136, July 25, 2024; full report:
[DOI OIG 2020-CR-066](https://www.oversight.gov/sites/default/files/oig-reports/DOI/Final-Evaluation-ReportNPS-Deferred-MaintenancePublic.pdf)).

Reporting units are not always one park:

- Sequoia and Kings Canyon are one unit (SEKI) in every source. The same SEKI
  figure is written on both the SEQU and KICA rows, and `method_note` says not
  to sum them.
- Kobuk Valley is reported inside Western Arctic National Parklands (WEAR),
  with Cape Krusenstern National Monument and Noatak National Preserve. The
  WEAR figure is written on the KOVA row and flagged.
- Eight of our parks are park-and-preserve units (Denali, Gates of the Arctic,
  Glacier Bay, Great Sand Dunes, Katmai, Lake Clark, Wrangell-St. Elias, New
  River Gorge). Every source reports each as one unit code, so the figure
  covers the park and the preserve together. We found no source that splits
  them.
- North Cascades includes Lake Chelan and Ross Lake National Recreation Areas.
  Big Bend includes the Rio Grande Wild and Scenic River. Virgin Islands
  National Park includes Virgin Islands Coral Reef National Monument in the
  FY2022 dataset. These notes come from the PDFs' unit lists and the FY2022
  dataset's "Additional Park Units Included in Calculations" column.
- Parks that cross state lines appear once per state in the PDFs
  (Yellowstone, Great Smoky Mountains) and the DOI workbook (Yellowstone,
  Great Smoky Mountains, Theodore Roosevelt). The script adds the state rows
  together.

**Changes over time.** These changes break comparisons across years. NPS and
its overseers do not agree on one date for the first change, so both readings
are given.

| First fiscal year affected | What changed | Source |
|---|---|---|
| FY2015 | DOI agencies stopped reporting a range (-15% to +25%) for industry-standard assets and moved to a single estimate. | [CRS R43997, Aug. 8, 2023](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2023-08-08_R43997_bf07042620da63674d52cf9af63d872a27cbd0e5.html), citing DOI's FY2014 financial report |
| FY2019 or FY2021 | NPS began adding design, compliance, construction management and project management costs to non-transportation assets. NPS says "Through 2018" it counted only construction costs ([FAQ](https://www.nps.gov/subjects/infrastructure/faqs.htm); [news release, May 9, 2022](https://www.nps.gov/orgs/1207/05-09-2022-dm-r.htm)), and CRS R43997 reads this as starting in FY2019. The DOI OIG and CRS R48136 date the change to FY2021: a "blanket" 35% markup (5% compliance, 17% design, 8% construction management, 5% project management) added about $3.7 billion of the $8.8 billion rise from FY2020 to FY2021. Roads and bridges already carried a 35% markup from the Federal Highway Administration (FHWA). | DOI OIG 2020-CR-066 (Sept. 2023); CRS R48136 |
| FY2022 | NPS moved from open work-order cost estimates to "parametric condition assessments": staff score parts of each asset 0 to 9, and a scoring tool converts the scores and the asset's replacement value into DM&R. The OIG says NPS began using it in the second quarter of FY2022 and expected full use at the end of FY2024. Concession-occupied and non-industry-standard assets stayed on work orders; FHWA assessments still cover paved roads, parking, bridges and tunnels. | DOI OIG 2020-CR-066; CRS R48136 (citing the NPS FY2024 and FY2025 budget justifications) |
| FY2023 | NPS changed which assets it counts, aligning with the Federal Real Property Profile (FRPP) it reports to the General Services Administration. The portfolio went from about 75,000 to about 72,000 assets. | CRS R48136 |
| FY2023 | FHWA's "road model update" for transportation assets "cumulatively account[s] for general inflation from recent years," and NPS says it raised paved-road DM&R 36% from FY2022 to FY2023. | CRS R48136 |
| FY2023 | From FY2023 on, NPS's own DM&R totals match DOI's. Before that, NPS figures included some concessioner-owned and other non-NPS assets that DOI's financial reports left out. | CRS R48136 (July 2024 and Oct. 2026 editions) |
| FY2025 | Assets marked for disposal (907 of 70,687 in FY2025) carry no DM&R in the total. | CRS R48136, Oct. 2026, citing the NPS FY2027 budget justification |

The data source changed too: FY2014-FY2022 figures come from the NPS Facility
Management Software System (FMSS); the FY2024 and FY2025 fact sheets say "The
data is from FRPP." Because of these changes, a rise or fall between FY2018 and
FY2022, or between FY2022 and FY2023, can reflect method as well as condition.
CRS writes that the portion of the decade's change due to methods "remains
unclear."

**Suppressed, censored or masked values.** None are suppressed. Rounding is the
masking that matters: the FY2024 and FY2025 NPS fact sheets print figures such
as "$9.6 M" or "$161 M," and CRS prints billions to three decimals. Where the
only copy is rounded, the processed file stores the rounded value and
`method_note` repeats the printed figure, for example "($161 M)."

**Missing data.** FY2019, FY2020 and FY2021 are missing for every park. FY2023
is missing for 54 of the 63 parks and FY2024 for 38. A missing row means no official park-level
figure was found, not zero. A published "$ -" or "-$" in the PDFs is a real
zero and is written as 0. The `locations` column is empty: NPS publishes
location counts by region only, not by park.

**Revisions.** NPS does not post revisions of past park figures. The one
revision we saw is the FY2023 NPS-wide and Intermountain Region totals
described above. Within each fiscal year, every archived copy we compared
agrees within rounding except as listed in `research/dm_overlap.md`. The FY2014
report exists in two dated versions (March 13 and October 20, 2015); the build
keeps the later one. We pin to the copies listed by URL and capture date in the
processed file.

**Units and rounding.** Nominal (current) US dollars of each fiscal year; NPS
does not adjust for inflation. CRS adjusts the national totals with the Bureau
of Economic Analysis price index for nondefense structures; we do not. Whole
dollars in FY2014-FY2018 and FY2022, cents in the DOI FY2025 workbook (rounded
to whole dollars here), $1 million for the CRS FY2023 values, and 2 or 3
significant figures for the FY2024 fact sheets. The whole-dollar precision is
far finer than the real accuracy, given the OIG findings above.

**Known quirks.**

- The FY2016 PDF's printed national total ($11,331,597,849) is $347,942 more
  than the sum of its own state totals. The national line matches CRS
  ($11.332 billion); the park rows are kept as printed.
- State subtotals in the PDFs can differ by a dollar or two from the sum of
  their rows, from rounding.
- The FY2022 park dataset sums to $22.222 billion, about $66 million (0.3%)
  less than the NPS-wide FY2022 figure CRS reports ($22.288 billion). The
  dataset lists parks and sites; we did not find which units account for the
  rest.
- The FY2025 fact sheet link for the National Park of American Samoa returns
  404 (2026-10-22). Its FY2025 value comes from the DOI workbook.
- The FY2022 park dataset stayed on nps.gov, unchanged, through at least May
  2026, under page text saying FY2023 numbers were "coming soon." The fact
  sheet PDFs linked from it in 2024 and 2025 (file names ending
  "-Fact-Sheet.pdf") also hold FY2022 data, rounded. They are not a separate
  year.
- Names differ by source ("Haleakal? National Park" in the DOI workbook,
  "Wrangell St Elias," "Great Sand Dunes National Monument and Preserve," "New
  River Gorge National River"). The script matches on normalized names and a

- DM&R is not the same as the money needed to fix a park. NPS lists other
  needs it leaves out: annual operating maintenance, life-cycle upkeep, and
  code and accessibility upgrades.

**Uncertainty.** NPS publishes no error bars. The DOI OIG (September 2023)
found work orders that were not entered, classified or closed consistently,
which "in some cases understated and in others overstated" the estimates, and
found that NPS lacked documentation for the size of the 35% markup. The OIG
did not test the new condition-assessment method. Through FY2014 DOI reported
industry-standard assets as a range of -15% to +25%, which is a rough guide to
the spread NPS itself expected then.

**How the Great American Outdoors Act (GAOA) relates.** GAOA (P.L. 116-152,
August 4, 2020) created the National Parks and Public Land Legacy Restoration
Fund. It received up to $1.9 billion a year for FY2021-FY2025, and NPS received
70%, or up to $1.33 billion a year (CRS R48136). The fund pays for projects
that address DM&R. It does not change how DM&R is measured. When a funded
project is finished, DOI says "the associated asset records are updated to
retire, or remove, the reported DM&R" (DOI DM&R page). Many projects take
several years, so funded work may not show in a park's DM&R until later. NPS
estimated in August 2025 that the FY2021-FY2025 projects would address $4.7
billion in DM&R once complete (CRS R48136, Oct. 2026). GAOA also required DOI
to publish project lists, which is one reason the park fact sheets and the DOI
DM&R page exist. The fiscal years of GAOA funding (FY2021 on) overlap the
method changes above, so year-to-year changes cannot be split between funding
and method from these data alone.

## Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers (BLS)

**What it is.** CPI-U, all items, U.S. city average, not seasonally adjusted, series CUUR0000SA0. It is a monthly index of consumer prices, with 1982-84 = 100. We use it only to turn each fiscal year's budget into FY2025 dollars.

**Where it comes from.** The BLS Public Data API v2 (https://api.bls.gov/publicAPI/v2/timeseries/data/), pulled by `scripts/fetch/cpi.py` with the key loaded by `d4tp_env.py`. Raw responses are cached in `data/raw/bls/`. No login is needed beyond the free API key.

**Version and vintage.** We pulled it on 2026-10-22 and cover January 1978 to December 2025. The not seasonally adjusted CPI-U is not revised after release.

**Coverage.** Urban consumers, about 90% of the U.S. population according to BLS. It measures consumer prices, not the costs the Park Service pays. A budget adjusted by CPI-U shows buying power in consumer terms, which is a common choice but not the only one.

**Changes over time.** BLS updates the market basket and methods from time to time. We know of no break that affects a fiscal-year average comparison over 2008 to 2025.

**Suppressed, censored or masked values.** None.

**Missing data.** October 2025 has no value. Its BLS footnote reads: "Data unavailable due to the 2025 lapse in appropriations." Our fiscal-year averages use October to September, so FY2025 (October 2024 to September 2025) is complete. The calendar 2025 average is not computed, and we do not use it.

**Revisions.** None for this series.

**Units and rounding.** An index level to three decimals. Fiscal-year averages are kept to four decimals in `data/processed/cpi.csv`.

**Known quirks.** The federal fiscal year starts October 1, so FY2025 starts in calendar 2024.

**Uncertainty.** BLS publishes standard errors for CPI changes. They are small next to the changes shown here, and we do not show them.

**License and attribution.** U.S. government work, in the public domain. Credit: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics.

## Park history

These sources feed `config/park_history.json`, a short list of dated events
for each of the 63 national parks: when each was first set aside, and when it
was established, renamed or redesignated. The page turns these into a short
summary under each park's name. `scripts/fetch/park_history.py` builds the
file. The event list for each park is picked by hand in that script. The
script stops if any quoted text is not found in the source, if a park has no
matching Index entry, or if an acreage row is missing. All downloads are
cached in `data/raw/history/`.

### The National Parks: Index 2012-2016 (National Park Service)

**What it is.** The official NPS list of every unit of the National Park
System, often called the "Red Book." Each entry gives the unit's name,
address, a short description, a history line, and acreage. The history line
lists, in plain text, when the unit was proclaimed, authorized, established,
renamed or redesignated, plus a list of boundary change dates.

**Where it comes from.** https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/upload/npindex2012-2016.pdf,
a 159-page PDF, about 6.5 MB, no login. It is linked from the NPS publications
page (https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/publications.htm). Cached as
`data/raw/history/npindex2012-2016.pdf`.

**Version and vintage.** The 2012-2016 edition. The server reports the file
was last changed on October 10, 2017. We looked for a newer edition and did
not find one. The NPS publications page links only this edition and a
2016-2017 addendum. The addendum link returned "not found" when we tried it on
October 22, 2026. It covers only units added after December 31, 2015, so it
would not change any of the 63 parks. We tried likely file names for newer
editions on nps.gov and found none. We could not reach the NPS History
eLibrary (npshistory.com) index pages we tried. A newer edition may exist that
we did not find.

**Coverage.** All 63 parks in `config/parks.json` have an entry. Events run
from 1832 (Hot Springs Reservation) to 2013 (Pinnacles). A few entries cover more than one
park or unit:
- Sequoia and Kings Canyon have separate entries, but both list the web
  address www.nps.gov/seki. The script picks each by its page and first words.
- The www.nps.gov/noca address appears on three entries: North Cascades
  National Park and two national recreation areas. Only the park entry is used.
- The www.nps.gov/grte address also appears on the John D. Rockefeller, Jr.
  Memorial Parkway entry. Only the Grand Teton entry is used.
- The park-and-preserve units (Denali, Gates of the Arctic, Glacier Bay, Great
  Sand Dunes, Katmai, Lake Clark, Wrangell-St. Elias) each have one entry with
  one history line for both parts.

**Changes over time.** Four parks changed name or type after this edition:
Gateway Arch (2018, then Jefferson National Expansion Memorial), Indiana Dunes
(2019, then a national lakeshore), White Sands (2019, then a national
monument) and New River Gorge (2020, then a national river). Those changes
come from the Recent Changes page below.

**Suppressed, censored or masked values.** None.

**Missing data.** Some entries give fewer dates than others. Isle Royale and
Virgin Islands give only an authorization date, with no separate
establishment date. For Isle Royale we added the 1940 establishment date from
another NPS document (see below). For Virgin Islands we did not find one and
left the gap. Many entries say only "a national monument" or "redesignated"
without naming the unit. In those cases the event's `name` joins the park's
name with the unit type, and `name_stated` is false. That is true for 20 of
the 142 events.

**Revisions.** The Index is reissued every few years, not revised in place. We
pin the 2012-2016 edition.

**Units and rounding.** Dates are written in the Index's own style, such as
"Feb. 26, 1919" and "March 4, 1929". We keep that as `date_text` and add an
ISO date.

**Known quirks.**
- The PDF's text layer is not clean. It drops or adds spaces inside words
  ("establis hed", "Nov . 10") and stores every hyphen as a soft hyphen, so
  line-break hyphens and real hyphens look the same. The `text` we keep is the
  source wording with those spacing and hyphen errors fixed. The script checks
  each `text` against the source with spaces and hyphens ignored, so a wrong
  word or date stops the build.
- One sentence can hold two events. Grand Teton's 1943 Jackson Hole National
  Monument and its 1950 enlargement both quote the same sentence.
- Zion has two different units named Zion National Monument (1918 and 1937),
  and Grand Canyon has two named Grand Canyon National Monument (1908 and
  1932).
- Bryce Canyon's entry says "renamed and redesignated Utah National Park June
  7, 1924" and "renamed Bryce Canyon National Park Feb. 25, 1928."
- The Index writes "Mt. McKinley National Park," "Haleakalā" with a macron,
  and "Hawai'i Volcanoes" with an okina in the heading. We do not know how the
  name was spelled in 1961, so that event uses "Hawaii Volcanoes National
  Park" and is marked `name_stated: false`.
- Kenai Fjords' Index acreage is printed as "669.983.65." We do not use Index
  acreage.

**What we left out.** Boundary change lists, wilderness dates, Biosphere
Reserve and World Heritage Site dates, Dark Sky Park dates, and El Portal
(Yosemite). We kept a boundary event only where the Index calls it out apart
from the list: Acadia's 1986 permanent boundary, Biscayne's 1980 enlargement,
Kings Canyon's 1940 renaming and enlargement, Grand Canyon's 1975
combination, Grand Teton's 1950 enlargement, Wind Cave's 1935 addition of the
game preserve, and Zion's 1956 addition of the second monument. We also kept
transfers from the U.S. Forest Service and dedication dates as `other`.

**Uncertainty.** The Index does not cite laws or proclamations. Where it
disagrees with Recent Changes, both dates are in `notes` and the event list
follows the Index.

**License and attribution.** U.S. government work, in the public domain.
Credit: National Park Service, The National Parks: Index 2012-2016.

### Recent Changes to the National Park System (National Park Service)

**What it is.** An NPS web page with tables of new units and changes to unit
designations, by date, with the law or proclamation and the NPS director at
the time.

**Where it comes from.** https://www.nps.gov/aboutus/recent-changes.htm, HTML,
no login. Cached as `data/raw/history/recent-changes.htm`.

**Version and vintage.** The page says "Last updated: September 23, 2025."
Downloaded October 22, 2026.

**Coverage.** The designation table goes back to 1994. We use it for four
events after the Index: Gateway Arch (February 22, 2018), Indiana Dunes
(February 15, 2019), White Sands (December 20, 2019) and New River Gorge
(December 27, 2020). For these, `text` joins the date, park and law cells of
the table row with " | ".

**Changes over time.** The page is edited in place. Older versions are not
posted.

**Suppressed, censored or masked values.** None.

**Missing data.** Rows before 1994 are not on the page.

**Revisions.** We keep the cached copy. A later download could differ.

**Units and rounding.** Dates are written out in full ("February 22, 2018").

**Known quirks.**
- The Gateway Arch row names the old unit "Jefferson Expansion Memorial"
  (missing "National") and cites "S. 1438" instead of a public law number.
- The Indiana Dunes row cites "P.L. 166-6" as printed.
- The New River Gorge row says "renamed from New River Gorge National River."
  We list it as `redesignated` because the unit type changed.
- Rows for other parks overlap the Index. They agree on dates for Black
  Canyon, Congaree, Cuyahoga Valley, Death Valley, Joshua Tree and Pinnacles.
  Two disagree:
  - Saguaro: the Index says "redesignated Oct. 4, 1994"; Recent Changes says
    October 14, 1994 (P.L. 103-364).
  - Great Sand Dunes: the Index gives the redesignation on Nov. 22, 2000 and
    establishment on Sept. 24, 2004; Recent Changes lists the redesignation on
    September 24, 2004 (P.L. 106-530).

**Uncertainty.** The page is a summary table. We did not check the laws
themselves.

**License and attribution.** U.S. government work, in the public domain.
Credit: National Park Service.

### Listing of Acreage, 09/30/2026 (NPS Land Resources Division)

**What it is.** A quarterly table of acreage for every NPS unit, split by
owner: NPS fee, NPS less than fee, other federal, other public and private.
We use the "Gross Area Acres" column, which is all land inside the
authorized boundary, whoever owns it.

**Where it comes from.** The NPS acreage reports page,
https://www.nps.gov/subjects/lwcf/acreagereports.htm. The file is
https://www.nps.gov/subjects/lwcf/upload/NPS-Acreage-09-30-2026.xlsx, an Excel
workbook, no login. Cached as `data/raw/history/NPS-Acreage-09-30-2026.xlsx`.
The script reads the first sheet, "Listing of Acreage."

**Version and vintage.** As of September 30, 2026, the newest report on the
page on October 22, 2026. Reports go back to at least 2011, mostly for the
end of each quarter or year.

**Coverage.** Every unit, keyed by a shortened upper-case name ("GREAT SMOKY
MTS NP," "T ROOSEVELT NP"). There is no park code column, so the script maps
codes to row names by hand.

**Changes over time.** Gross acres change as boundaries change. Mammoth Cave
is one large case: 72,041.73 gross acres in this report (20,034.11 of them
non-federal) against 52,830.19 in the 2012-2016 Index. The reports do not say
why. The 12/31/2025 and 06/30/2026 reports we checked show the same Mammoth
Cave figure.

**Suppressed, censored or masked values.** None.

**Missing data.** None of the 63 parks is missing a row.

**Revisions.** Each quarter replaces the last. We pin 09/30/2026.

**Units and rounding.** Acres, to two decimals as published.

**Known quirks.**
- Park and preserve on separate rows: Denali, Gates of the Arctic, Glacier
  Bay, Great Sand Dunes, Katmai, Lake Clark and Wrangell-St. Elias. For these,
  `acres` is the national park row only, `acres_preserve` is the preserve
  row, and `acres_park_and_preserve` is the sum.
- New River Gorge has one combined row, "NEW RIVER GORGE NP & PRES," so its
  park and preserve parts cannot be split. `acres` is the combined figure.
- Everglades is split into "EVERGLADES NP" and "EVERGLADES EXPANSION."
  Endnote 6 says to add them for the park's total, and `acres` is that sum.
- National Park of American Samoa is all "Other Public Acres." The Index says
  the land is leased.
- Gross area includes private and other non-federal land inside the
  boundary, which is large for some parks (for example Redwood, Channel
  Islands and Voyageurs).

**Uncertainty.** NPS does not publish error bars. Two-decimal precision is
finer than the accuracy of most boundary surveys.

**License and attribution.** U.S. government work, in the public domain.
Credit: National Park Service, Land Resources Division.
